Key Aspects of Investment Analysis
Investment analysis is a process that project management professionals use to assess the various factors involved in a project and determine the potential success of investment opportunities.

It’s like planning a road trip. Before setting out, consider the costs of gas, food, and accommodation (initial investment) and weigh them against the joy of the journey, the scenic views, and the time spent with loved ones (benefits).

You might compare different routes and destinations to find the best value and ensure the trip is worth the expense and effort. This careful planning helps you decide if the road trip will be enjoyable and memorable without overspending, much like investment analysis helps determine if a project will be profitable and beneficial.

Next, let's explore the key metrics involved in investment analysis to evaluate project viability, which is the likelihood that a project will be profitable and sustainable based on its financial, operational, and market factors.

Return on Investment (ROI)
The first metric focuses on measuring a project's return on investment (ROI). This measures the profit generated by an investment relative to its cost. There are two types of ROI measurements:

Anticipated ROI

Actual ROI

Anticipated ROI  
Anticipated ROI is calculated before a project starts using estimated costs and revenues. For PMPs, this helps determine whether a project is worth pursuing.

Consider the earlier analogy of planning a road trip. While completing your budget and itinerary, you notice that the cost is relatively high, and you’ll only be able to visit one tourist attraction. Due to the high accommodation costs, you can only invite one person. Instead of going on the road trip, you host a themed dinner party for all your loved ones, where everyone brings a unique dish to enjoy.

Without completing your budget, you wouldn't have been able to make this decision, potentially leading to a regrettable experience.

Actual ROI
While Anticipated ROI looks at the costs before a project starts, Actual ROI calculates the final costs and revenue after a project concludes. Actual ROI shows the true return of the investment compared to estimates.

Let’s imagine you completed the road trip, only to discover afterward that you spent far more money during the trip and had fewer memorable experiences due to budget limitations. In this case, the actual costs outweigh the benefits of completing the trip.

Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)
The second metric, cost-benefit analysis (CBA), is a calculated process to evaluate the costs and benefits of a specific project or decision.

For example, when deciding whether to buy a new smartphone, you consider the cost of the phone and compare it to the benefits, such as better camera quality, faster performance, and new features. If the benefits of the new device outweigh the cost, you'll go ahead and make the purchase. If not, you might keep your current device or look for a more affordable option. This helps you make an informed decision based on the value you’ll get compared to your spending.

There are a few steps to complete the cost-benefit analysis process effectively:

Step 1: Define the goals and objectives of the proposed project.

This step focuses on establishing what needs to be accomplished for the pursuit of it to be considered successful. It is a crucial step to complete as it will assist you in identifying the costs and benefits associated with the project.

Step 2: Calculate the total of all costs related to the project.

As a PMP, it is vital not to exclude any costs related to the project when conducting your CBA. In step 2, you’ll need to identify all direct expenses related to the project. Direct expenses in project management could include marketing, supplies, travel expenses, licensing fees, and even equipment costs.

Step 3: Calculate the projected benefits.

In step 3, you must highlight the benefits of pursuing a specific project. This information will be used in step 4 to determine whether the benefits outweigh the cost or vice versa. These benefits are seen as positive outcomes and can be financial, increased efficiency, or improved customer satisfaction.

Step 4: Subtract the total costs from the total benefits.

In step 4, you will use the information gathered in steps 2 and 3 to determine if the project is worth pursuing. You will subtract the project's total costs (step 2) from the total benefits (step 3) to determine if the benefits outweigh the costs.

Present Value (PV)
In PMP, present value is the current worth of a future amount of money, discounted at a specific rate. It helps determine how much a future cash flow is worth today.

Formula:

Present Value = Future Cash Flow / (1 + Discount Rate)^Number of Periods

The future cash flow is the amount of money expected to be received in the future, while the discount rate is the rate of return used to account for the time value of money, usually expressed in a percentage. The number of periods is the period over which the cash flow is expected to be received. For example, if it were over five years, the number of periods would be 5.

Point of Total Assumption (PTA)
The financial concept called the point of total assumption is used to identify when an entity assumes full financial obligation for a project. It is the point at which the project's investment by the organization or investor matches its completion cost.

Formula:

Point of Total Assumption = (Costs Incurred + Estimated Costs to Complete) / Total Value of Project

Future Value (FV)
In project management, future value refers to the value of an asset at a future time. This financial metric measures an investment's potential value at a given point in the future.

Formula:

Future Value = Payment \* (1 + Compounding Rate)^Number of Periods

(FV) is the future value of the investment.

(P) is the initial payment (principal).

(r) is the compounding interest rate per period.

(n) is the number of compounding periods.

Standard Deviation (SD)
PMPs use standard deviation to measure the degree to which the individual values in a dataset differ from the mean or average of the dataset.

Formula:

Standard Deviation = √(S(X - Mean)^2 / N)

(X) is an individual value in the dataset.

(Mean) is the mean of the dataset.

(S) represents the sum of all values in the dataset.

(N) expresses the number of values in the dataset.

The Net Present Value (NPV) is a Return on Investment (ROI) analysis that determines a monetary value for the accumulated cost of the investment and benefits of a project over a set time.

Expected Monetary Value (EMV)
Expected Monetary Value (EMV) evaluates potential risks and aids PMPs in making informed decisions.

Formula:

Expected Monetary Value = probability of outcome \* the potential impact of outcome

The probability of the outcome is expressed as a decimal between 0 and 1, and the potential impact is the monetary value of the outcome that can be either positive or negative.

For example, a cost overrun for a project is $50,000, and the probability of this risk occurring is 30%.

EMV = 0.30 \* $50,000 = $15,000

This indicates that the expected value of the risk is a cost of $15,000.

With an EMV analysis, you can prioritize risk management activities and allocate resources accordingly to better manage the project.

Internal Rate of Return (IRR)
In project management, the Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is a crucial metric to evaluate a project's profitability. IRR is the percentage rate at which the project's Net Present Value (NPV) is zero. Essentially, it represents the expected annual return on investment for the project.

Example

Imagine being part of a company considering investing in a new software development project.

The finance team calculates that this project's IRR is 12%; in other words, it is expected to generate a 12% annual return on investment (ROI).

However, the company's required rate of return, or the minimum return they expect from any investment, is 10%. Since the IRR of 12% is higher than the required rate of return of 10%, the project is deemed viable and likely to be profitable. Therefore, the company would likely decide to proceed with the software development project.

From this example, IRR aids Project Management Professionals in determining whether a project is worth investing in by comparing it to the expected minimum return. If the IRR is higher, the project is considered a good investment.

Net Present Value (NPV)
Net Present Value (NPV) is a method of Return on Investment (ROI) analysis that assigns a monetary value to a project's total costs and benefits over a specific period.

Using an ROI analysis, you can measure the profitability of an investment, which will, in turn, assist you in making informed financial decisions. It is calculated by dividing the net profit of the investment by its initial cost using this formula:

ROI = (Net Profit / Initial Cost) x 100

Let’s look at an example of how to do this calculation.

Example

Imagine you invested $10,000 in a marketing campaign for your small business. Over the year, the campaign generated $15,000 in additional sales. The costs associated with the campaign, including materials and labor, amounted to $3,000.

First, you will need to calculate the net profit as shown below:

Net Profit = Total Income - Associated Costs

Net Profit = $15,000 - $3,000

Net Profit = $12,000

Once you have established the net profit, you can calculate the ROI:

ROI = (Net Profit / Initial Cost) x 100

ROI = ($12,000 / $10,000) x 100

ROI = 120%

This means your investment in the marketing campaign yielded a 120% return.

Before you can calculate the IRR of a specific investment, it is crucial to understand the concept of Return on Investment and how it is used to determine net present value. Most formulas you need to know as a Project Management Professional are based on ROI.

Relationship between IRR and NPV
The Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is directly related to Net Present Value (NPV). IRR is the percentage at which the NPV equals zero. Always remember that NPV is a value, while IRR is a percentage.

When the IRR matches the required rate of return, the NPV is zero. The required rate of return is the return an investor or organization expects from a particular investment or project.

So, if the IRR equals the required rate of return, the NPV will be zero. For a project to be viable, the IRR must be greater than the required rate of return. If the IRR is less, the project is unlikely to proceed.

Example

A company is considering investing in a new project. They calculate the IRR to be 8%. This means that at an 8% return, the Net Present Value (NPV) of the project is zero.

The company's required rate of return for any project is 10%. Since the IRR (8%) is less than the required rate of return (10%), the NPV would be negative. This indicates that the project will not meet the company's investment expectations and will likely result in a loss.

In this scenario, the company would likely decide not to proceed with the project because the IRR does not meet its required rate of return, illustrating the important relationship between IRR and NPV.

Key differences between IRR and NPV
The difference between Net Present Value (NPV) and Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is in how they evaluate investment profitability.

NPV is a metric that calculates the difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows over a period of time.

It represents the difference in the currency amount between the present value of discounted cash inflows and outflows over a specific period of time. If a project’s NPV is above zero, it’s considered financially worthwhile.

In contrast, IRR is the calculation used to estimate the profitability of potential investments using a percentage value rather than a currency amount (e.g., dollar, euro, sterling).

In summary, the IRR is the rate of return at which the NPV of all the cash flows (both positive and negative) from a project or investment equals zero.

Key takeaways for IRR and NPV in assessing an investment
NPV provides a currency value representing the additional value an investment will generate, considering the time value of money, while IRR gives a percentage return that helps compare the profitability of different investments or projects.

NPV is more reliable when comparing projects of different sizes or durations, as it considers the absolute profitability in terms of currency.

While NPV gives you the projected increase in wealth from an investment, IRR tells you the rate at which each dollar or euro (the currency) invested in a project is expected to grow during the investment period.

NPV is the sum of all cash flows at a discounted rate of return that reflects the risk of the investment and IRR measures the rate of return of an investment based on the cash flows generated by the project.

Conclusion
In summary, investors should use NPV and IRR to assess the profitability of a potential project as a good rule of thumb before deciding on the investment.

Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)
Benefits Cost Ratio = Total Benefits / Total Costs

The total benefit is referred to as the sum of all the positive outcomes or impacts of a project, including:

financial benefits, e.g., increased revenues or cost savings

non-financial benefits, e.g., improved environmental outcomes

The total cost is the sum of all the resources required to complete a project, including direct and indirect costs.

If the result shows a Benefit-Cost Ratio (BCR) greater than 1, the project's benefits outweigh the costs, making it a good investment. In contrast, if the BCR is less than 1, it might not be a good investment as the project’s costs outweigh the benefits.

Project A: Cost-Benefit Ratio = $180,000 / $100,000 = 1.8

The calculation has shown that Project A has a higher cost-benefit ratio of 1.8 compared to Project B's 1.67, making Project A the more efficient option in terms of benefits per dollar spent.

Total Present Value of Cash Flows: $49,737

NPV: $49,737 - $50,000 = -$263

The NPV is -$263, indicating that the project would result in a small net loss and may not be a good investment.

Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) overview
A thorough understanding of the DCF formulas is essential for project management professionals. This knowledge will enable you to estimate the intrinsic value of an investment or business by discounting future cash flows to their present value.

The intrinsic value of an investment or business is a measure of what an asset is worth based on its underlying characteristics and properties, such as cash flows and financial performance. This differs from the market price of an asset.

Key DCF formulas
Present Value (PV)
PV is a financial metric used to calculate the value of future cash flows in today’s currency. It’s calculated by discounting the expected future cash flows back to their current value, considering the time value of money.

Formula:
PV=(1+r)tCF

Where:

(CF) represents the future cash flow.

(r) is the discount rate or the required rate of return.

(t) is the time period (typically expressed in years).

Calculating the present value (PV) of an investment helps evaluate its profitability and compare different investment opportunities easily, even with varying cash flows and timelines. PV aids decision-makers in making informed choices by accounting for the opportunity cost of capital and investment risk. It enables project leads to make financially sound decisions by considering the time value of money and associated risks.

Payback Period
The payback period determines how long it takes for an investment to recover its initial cost. It’s a simple method that doesn’t consider the time value of money.

Add the investment's positive cash flows over time to calculate the payback period. Subtract each cash flow from the initial investment until the cumulative total becomes positive. The payback period is when these cumulative cash flows will equal or exceed the initial investment, indicating when the investment has been fully recovered.

Picture a scenario where you invest in a business venture or buy a piece of equipment. The payback period helps you determine when you’ll start making a profit and recover your invested money. This may raise the question, “How quickly will I get my money back?”

The payback period is useful for assessing liquidity (how easily assets can be converted into cash) and short-term financial feasibility (whether a project is viable).

If the payback period is short, it suggests that the investment will pay off quickly, which can be suitable for managing cash flow and meeting short-term financial requirements.

However, the payback period lacks precision and ignores the long-term value of cash flows.

For example, a project might have a short payback period but generate substantial profits over many years. Ignoring these long-term benefits could lead to suboptimal decisions. While the payback period provides a quick snapshot, other metrics (like net present value or internal rate of return) are vital for a more comprehensive evaluation.

Net Present Value (NPV)
Net Present Value evaluates an investment's profitability by comparing the present value of inflows (benefits) to the present value of outflows (costs). It is also part of the set of Discounting Cash Flow (DCF) methods.

Formula:

[ NPV = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1 + r)^t} - C_0 ]

Where:

(CF_t) represents the cash flow in years (t).

(n) is the total number of years.

(C_0) is the initial investment.

A positive NPV indicates that the investment generates value, while a negative NPV suggests that the project may not be worthwhile.

NPV is highly valuable in project management as it assesses the long-term financial viability of potential investments by considering the time value of money. This approach offers a more accurate perspective than simply analyzing raw accounting figures. NPV compares investment alternatives, evaluates project profitability, and calculates benefit-cost ratios, aiding informed investment decisions. It is crucial for project leads to evaluate investment opportunities and make financially sound choices.

Internal Rate of Return (IRR)
The Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is the discount rate at which an investment's NPV becomes zero and represents the project’s effective interest rate.

This method can be used to measure your investment’s profitability. Imagine that you’re planting a money tree. The IRR would be the growth rate at which the tree needs to grow so that the money you harvest equals the cost of planting it.

You'll enjoy a profit if the tree grows faster than this rate. It’s similar to finding the break-even point for the growth of your investment.

To calculate an investment's IRR, you will follow three simple steps. First, you’ll need to estimate all future cash flows expected from the investment. Then, you will choose an initial discount rate to calculate the present value of these cash flows. Finally, you will calculate the NPV of these cash flows using the formula below.

Formula:

NPV=t=1∑n(1+r)tCFt−Initial Investment

Where:

(CF_t) is the cash flow at a time (t).

(r) is the discount rate.

(n) is the total number of periods.

To complete your calculation, you must adjust the discount rate and repeat the calculation until the NPV is zero. The discount rate that achieves this is the IRR for your investment.

It is important to remember that the initial investment amount will be indicated as negative, as this refers to money that has already been paid out—in other words, your outflow. Subsequently, cash flows can either be positive or negative.

IRR essentially helps assess an investment's attractiveness. If the IRR exceeds the required rate of return, then the project is considered viable.

Calculating IRR can be complex because it involves trial and error to find the rate that sets the NPV to zero. Spreadsheet software like Excel has built-in functions to calculate IRR seamlessly.

IRR is a crucial tool for PMPs to assess the financial viability, compare the profitability, and effectively communicate the potential success of projects. IRR ultimately supports PMPs in the following ways:

Evaluate project proposals: They assess the project's attractiveness by comparing IRR with the required rate of return.

Prioritize projects: Naturally, PMPs will be able to determine which projects return a higher IRR value and prioritize these types of projects accordingly.

Assess any project-associated risk: IRR calculations help understand how cash flow changes impact the IRR.

Make informed decisions: Completing an IRR analysis is a useful tool for informing and guiding project selection and resource allocation.

DCF scenario
As a Project Management Professional (PMP), you're tasked with evaluating a new pharmaceutical manufacturing facility proposal to meet increasing product demand.

To make an informed decision, you'll use Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis, focusing on key metrics like Present Value (PV), Payback Period, Net Present Value (NPV), and Internal Rate of Return (IRR).

Your goal is to determine if the facility is a sound investment by calculating the present value of future cash flows, estimating the payback period, and evaluating overall profitability through NPV. You'll also assess the expected rate of return using IRR to compare it against the company's required rate and other opportunities.

By analyzing these financial metrics, you'll provide insights to stakeholders, helping them understand the project's potential benefits and risks supporting a data-driven decision on whether to proceed with the facility.

The project is expected to span over five years. The initial investment required is $15 million.

Projected Cash Flows:

Year 1: $1 million

Year 2: $2 million

Year 3: $5 million

Year 4: $5 million

Year 5: $7 million

First, you will calculate the present value of these cash flows by making use of the below DCF formula:

[ PV = \frac{CF}{(1 + r)^t} ]

Where:

(PV) represents the present value.

(CF) is the cash flow for a given year.

(r) represents the discount rate (i.e., the target rate of return).

(t) indicates the time period.

The discount rate depends on your company’s cost of capital or desired return and can usually be obtained from the company’s Finance department.

For this scenario, let’s assume the discount rate is 10%.

Step 1: You will use the projected cash flows for each year to calculate the present value:

PV of Year 1 cash flow: (1+0.10)11,000,000=$909,090.91

PV of Year 2 cash flow: (1+0.10)22,000,000=$1,652,892.56

PV of Year 3 cash flow: (1+0.10)35,000,000=$3,791,139.24

PV of Year 4 cash flow: (1+0.10)45,000,000=$3,305,785.12

PV of Year 5 cash flow: (1+0.10)57,000,000=$4,042,553.19

Step 2: Then, you will calculate the total amount for the cash flows over five years to determine the DCF as shown below:

DCF = Year 1($909,090.91) + Year 2 ($1,652,892.56) + Year 3 ($3,791,139.24) + Year 4 (3,305,785.12) + Year 5 ($4,042,553.19).

DCF = $14,080,074.01

Step 3: Once you have calculated the DCF, you will use this amount to calculate the net present value (NPV) by using the formula below:

NPV = DCF - Initial Investment

NPV = DCF ($14,080,074.01) - Initial investment ($15,000,000)

NPV = $-919,925.99

Since the NPV is negative, this project may not be a favorable investment. You might want to reconsider or adjust the discount rate to evaluate further.

In this example, DCF helps assess whether the new pharmaceutical manufacturing facility project is a good investment. It considers the time value of money and provides valuable insights for decision-making.

Investment Analysis
What is investment analysis?
Investment analysis embodies the principles and methods of making investment mix and policy decisions, matching investments to objectives, and balancing risk against performance. It is about ensuring you have ticked all these boxes about your investment potential!

As a project lead, you must understand investment analysis when reviewing a project proposal. This will assist you in answering the question of whether or not a project is worth the investment.

The purpose of investment analysis
The primary purpose of investment analysis is to evaluate potential projects or investments to determine their viability and alignment with strategic goals. This involves assessing risks, benefits, costs, and returns to make informed decisions on resource allocation. The analysis helps ensure that investments are well-balanced, align with overall objectives, and maximize potential returns while managing risk effectively.

Investment analysis aims to measure how well an investment will fit into a company or portfolio.

Here are the key points to remember about why you would conduct an investment analysis and its purpose:

Investment analysis involves researching and evaluating an industry to predict its future performance and determine its suitability for a specific investment or project.

An investment analysis may also involve evaluating or creating an overall financial strategy.

Before conducting an investment analysis, you may choose from various types to achieve the most suitable results. The main types of investment analysis include bottom-up, top-down, fundamental, and technical.

Next, you will learn more about the common types of investment analysis listed above in the key points, along with examples of how they differ.

Types of investment analysis
Fundamental analysis
This analysis focuses on the company’s financial health and intrinsic value. Intrinsic value is a financial term that refers to the perceived or calculated value of an asset, investment, or company. It will also consider factors like brand reputation, trademarks, and copyrights.

Assume that you are involved in this step as part of the leadership team conducting the investment analyses. To do the fundamental analysis, you would start by reviewing the company's financial statements or assessing its growth potential.

Technical analysis
A technical analysis examines stock price patterns using charts and trading signals. Analysts pay attention to price movements that indicate a certain pattern or trading signals and use charts or tools to help identify the strengths and weaknesses of securities.

In this case, one might conduct a SWOT analysis. SWOT analysis is a strategic planning and management technique used to help identify Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats related to business competition or project planning.

Differences between technical and fundamental analysis
Technical analysis differs from fundamental analysis in that a stock's price and volume are the only inputs or metrics used. There is an assumption that all publicly known fundamentals have factored into price; therefore, it is considered that there is no need to pay close attention to them.

Top-Down analysis
The top-down analysis considers macroeconomic (large-scale or general economic factors) factors and market trends. This analysis uses broader economic trends, including industry sectors and market cycles, to narrow specific investment opportunities.

Bottom-Up analysis
This is specific to companies and their metrics. This focuses on individual stocks, evaluating their value, management competence, pricing power, and unique characteristics. It doesn’t consider economic or market cycles.

Portfolio analysis
Portfolio analysis evaluates investments within a portfolio. Measuring a portfolio's performance involves monitoring and analyzing its two primary measures: return and risk.

Security analysis
This type of investment analysis focuses on individual securities, such as stocks and bonds.

Key aspects of investment analysis formulas
Let's unpack the key metrics or formulas in investment analysis to assess project viability. You will need to understand these concepts to evaluate project proposals effectively.

Return on Investment (ROI)
ROI measures the profit generated by an investment relative to its cost. There are two types of ROI:

Anticipated ROI  
This ROI type is typically calculated before a project starts using estimated costs and revenues. Furthermore, anticipated ROI serves as a guide in deciding whether a project is worth pursuing.

Actual ROI
Actual ROI is calculated after a project concludes using final costs and revenues. The actual ROI shows the true return compared to estimates.

Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)
A cost-benefit analysis estimates costs and benefits to determine project profitability.

Each financial metric provides a different perspective on the financial health and potential profitability of the potential project or investment.

Net Present Value (NPV)
NPV is the difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows over a period of time.

Internal Rate of Return (IRR)
The IRR is the discount rate that makes the NPV of all the cash flows from a particular project equal to zero. This metric evaluates the attractiveness of a potential project or investment.

Payback Period
This refers to the time it will take for the cash inflows from a project to cover or pay back the initial investment. It doesn’t consider the time value of money as some other calculations do.
