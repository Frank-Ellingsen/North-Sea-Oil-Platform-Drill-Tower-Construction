In project management, the discipline of schedule management holds a prominent role. Despite the evolving methodologies and tools at a project managers’ disposal, the importance of managing schedules, and time, remains.

This reading will explore the methods of schedule development and formulas used by project managers to monitor and control project schedules.

Time and schedule management
Schedule management involves planning, developing, managing, and controlling project timelines to ensure that all project activities are completed within the defined period. It encompasses identifying tasks, estimating duration, defining dependencies, and allocating resources to ensure timely project completion.

Through the use of various techniques and formulas, project managers can monitor progress, anticipate delays, and make informed decisions to keep the project on track.

Schedule development
Choosing a schedule development methodology is influenced by several factors, tailored to the preferences and needs of various project stakeholders. The type of schedule chosen can significantly impact how effectively the project is managed and communicated. Some key contributing factors include:

Stakeholder requirements

Project complexity

Project size and duration

Resource availability

Project management tools and software

Regulatory and compliance requirements

Tools, techniques and methods of schedule development
Let’s explore some of the most commonly used methods to develop schedules.

1. Gantt Chart
   The Gantt Chart is a bar chart that represents a project schedule by illustrating the start and finish dates of elements of the project. It is widely used due to its simplicity and visual clarity.

Each task is represented by a horizontal bar, and the position and length of the bar reflects the start date, duration, and end date of the task. Gantt charts assist in planning and coordinating tasks, understanding dependencies, and tracking the progress of the project.

To create a Gantt chart you start by listing all the activities or tasks involved in the project. Each task is then assigned a start date and an estimated duration. These tasks are then plotted on the chart with bars extending from the start date to the end date. Dependencies between tasks are often indicated with lines connecting the bars. Gantt charts are useful for small to medium sized projects where the number of tasks and dependencies can be visually managed with ease. They provide a clear overview of the project timeline and help identify potential delays and bottlenecks.

However, for larger projects, Gannt charts can become cumbersome and may require simplification or the use of additional tools to manage their complexity.

2. Milestone chart
   A milestone chart is a timeline that highlights major events, milestones, or critical points in a project. Unlike Gantt charts, which detail every task, milestones charts focus only on key deliverables or phases.

Each milestone represents a significant point of progress, such as the completion of the project phase, a major decision point, or the achievement of critical goals.

Milestone charts are created by identifying key points in the project that mark significant progress or decisions. These milestones are plotted on a timeline, providing a clear and concise visual summary of the project’s critical events. Each milestone is typically labeled with a brief description and a target date.

Milestone charts are highly effective for communication with stakeholders, as they provide a quick snapshot of the project’s progress and upcoming critical points. They help ensure that all team members and stakeholders are aligned on major goals and timeline.

Moreover, milestone charts do not provide detailed task level information, so they are often used in conjunction with other details scheduling tools like Gantt charts or network diagrams.

3. Critical Path Method (CPM)
   The critical path method (CPM) is a network diagram technique used to analyze, plan, and schedule complex projects. It identifies the sequence of tasks that directly affect the project completion time, known as the critical path. This path determines the shortest possible duration to complete the project, and highlights tasks that if delayed will affect the overall project timeline.

To create a CPM diagram, you list all project activities along with their dependencies and estimated duration. These activities are then represented as nodes, or arrows, in a network diagram, showing the sequence and interdependencies. The longest path through this network, with the least amount of float , is the critical path.

The CPM is valuable for understanding the project timeline and identifying which tasks are critical for on-time completion. The CPM helps project managers prioritize tasks, allocate resources efficiently, and monitor progress closely.

However, creating and maintaining CPM diagrams can be time-consuming, especially for large projects with many interdependencies. They are best suited for projects where precise scheduling and control of timeline are crucial.

4. Program Evaluation and Review Technique (PERT)
   The PERT is a statistical tool used to analyze and represent tasks involved in completing a given project. PERT helps in planning and scheduling complex projects by estimating the time required to complete tasks and identifying the minimum time needed to complete the entire project.

PERT uses three different estimates for each task:

Optimistic timing: The shortest time in which the task can be completed if everything goes better than expected.

Most likely timing: The best estimate of the time required to complete the task, assuming everything proceeds as normal.

Pessimistic timing: The longest time the task might take if things go wrong.

PERT charts are particularly useful for managing large and complex projects with uncertain activity durations. They help project managers identify critical tasks, estimate project duration, and analyze the impact of potential delays.

By providing an estimated view of project timelines, PERT enables better risk management and more informed decision-making.

5. Simulation method
   For projects with many uncertainties, the simulation method is a powerful tool to test assumptions and understand risks, gaps, and opportunities with minimal impact on the live project. By creating multiple schedule simulations, project managers can evaluate potential outcomes based on evolving estimates and assumptions.

The simulation method involves:

Creating multiple schedule simulations to test various scenarios to see how different factors might affect the project timeline.

Use simulations to validate the accuracy of time and resource estimates, and identify potential risks and gaps.

Continuously update simulations throughout the project to gain insights into managing the overall schedule as work progresses.

Simulation provides a dynamic approach to project scheduling, allowing project managers to explore different scenarios and develop strategies to mitigate risks. This method is particularly useful for managing schedules with many unknowns, as it helps anticipate potential issues and make informed decisions to keep the project on track.

6. Schedule compression
   This tool helps reduce the overall duration of a project by decreasing the time allocated to specific activities. It enables project managers to meet time constraints while maintaining the original project scope. Two methods can be used for this purpose:

Crashing: Crashing is a schedule compression technique used to expedite project completion by allocating additional resources. This may involve securing more personnel, using specialized software, approving overtime pay, or acquiring external materials. By intensifying resource application within a shorter period, tasks are completed faster than initially estimated. While effective, crashing can increase costs and should be used cautiously to avoid compromising quality.

Fast-tracking: Fast-tracking is another schedule compression technique that reduces the project timeline by performing tasks concurrently. Instead of following a sequential order, tasks are overlapped or stacked to coincide, which accelerates project progress. Fast-tracking can shorten the schedule significantly but may introduce risks related to task dependencies and coordination. Therefore, it requires careful planning and monitoring to mitigate potential issues.

7. Resource leveling
   Resource leveling is a scheduling technique aimed at optimizing resources to achieve the best possible project outcomes. This method ensures that all assigned resources are utilized consistently, avoiding periods of inactivity or overactivity.

By balancing resource allocation, project costs can be better managed, and the project schedule can stay on track. Resource leveling is often used alongside other scheduling techniques to enhance cost efficiency and schedule adherence.

8. Computer applications
   Computer applications play a crucial role in modern schedule management by automating and streamlining the planning, execution, monitoring and control of project schedules. Project management software offers a variety of scheduling functionalities that assist project managers in efficiently handling complex projects. Some key benefits of using computer applications are:

Software automates the scheduling process, reducing the time and efforts required to create and maintain schedules.

Tools like Gantt charts, PERT diagrams, and network diagrams provide visual representation of project timeline, dependencies, and progress.

Applications help in assigning resources effectively, ensuring optimal use without overloading or underutilizing any resource.

Simulation features allow project managers to test various scenarios and assess the impact of potential changes.

Software care provides real-time updates and notifications, ensuring that all stakeholders are aware of any changes or delays.

May software applications integrate with other tools and platforms, enabling seamless data flow and collaboration across different project management aspects.

Comprehensive report and dashboard functionalities offer detailed insights into project status, performance metrics, and key milestones. Dashboards provide a visual summary of critical project information, making it easier to communicate with stakeholders and monitor progress.

Conclusion
Schedule management is an indispensable facet of project management ensuring that projects are completed on time and within scope. By leveraging a range of tools and methodologies like Gantt charts, PERT, CPM, and simulation, project managers can develop, monitor, and control schedules effectively. Moreover the integration of computer applications further enhances the ability to manage complex projects by automating processes, providing visual representations, optimizing resource allocation, and enabling real-time updates and collaboration.

Schedule Management Formulas
In project management, schedule management formulas are mathematical tools used to plan, analyze, and control a project's timeline. These formulas help project managers estimate task durations, identify the critical path, assess schedule performance, and make informed decisions to keep the project on track. Let’s explore these formulas in detail.

Schedule variance (SV)
Schedule variance (SV) is a metric that indicates whether a project is ahead of or behind its planned schedule. It is calculated by comparing the earned value (EV), the work performed, against the planned value (PV) of the scheduled work.

Before looking at these formulas, let’s explore EV and PV in the context of schedule variance.

Earned Value (EV)is measured in terms of budgeted money for the work that has been performed. It is typically expressed as a percentage of work completed relative to the budget. EV is also known as Budgeted Cost of Work Performed (BCWP).

Planned Value (PV) represents the budgeted cost of work that has been authorized for a scheduled activity or work breakdown structure (WBS) component during a specific time period or project phase. These budgets are established during the planning processes and serve as benchmarks for project performance. Sum of all PVs equals Budget at Completion (BAC), which is the total planned budget for the project. PV is also known as Budgeted Cost of Work Scheduled (BCWS).

Schedule variance (SV) = EV - PV

Schedule variance (SV) = BCWP - BCWS

SV < 0: (negative value) Indicates that project is behind the schedule.

SV > 0: (positive value) indicates that the project is ahead of schedule.

SV = 0: indicates that project is on schedule.

Schedule performance index (SPI)
SPI measures the schedule progress to date against the planned progress. It is a ratio that measures the efficiency of time utilization in a project. It compares the Earned Value (EV) of the work performed to the Planned Value (PV) of the work scheduled.

Schedule Performance Index (SPI) = EV/PV

SPI > 1: Indicates the project is ahead of schedule.

SPI < 1: Indicates the project is behind schedule.

SPI = 1: Indicates the project is on schedule.

Critical path analysis formulas
Critical path analysis is a technique used to determine the sequence of critical activities that dictates the overall project duration. Through the employment of forward and backward pass calculations, it helps identify the longest path of tasks from start to finish, known as critical path. This section highlights the typical formulas used in the critical path method.

1. Forward pass calculations
   In the forward pass, the Early Start (ES) and Early Finish (EF) values for each activity along with overall project duration are calculated. To streamline the calculation, the “end of the day” notation is applied to both early start (ES) and late start (LS) values.

This approach sets the start of the network diagram as the “end of day zero”.The calculation process initiates by assigning a zero the ES of the first activity.

Subsequent calculations utilize the following formula:

Start node:

Begin with the project’s initial activity. Set its ES to zero.

EF is calculated by adding the duration of the activity to the ES.

Early Finish (EF) = ES + Activity duration

Subsequent activities:

For activities following the initial one, the ES is set to the EF of the preceding activity.

Early Start (ES) = Maximum EF value from the immediate predecessor

2. Backward pass calculations
   The second step of the calculation is backward pass. Through this step, Late Start (LS) and Late Finish (LF) values are computed. As the name suggests, the calculation starts at the last activity in schedule and proceeds backward through the schedule until the Late Start (LS) value is calculated for the schedule’s first activity.

The formulas are shown below:

End node:

Begin with the final activity and set its late finish (LF) to the project’s expected completion time.

The LS is calculated by subtracting the duration from the LF.

Late Start (LS) = LF - Duration

Preceding activities:

For activities leading to the final one, the LF is set to the LS of the subsequent activity.

Late Finish (LF) = Minimum LS value from the immediate successors

3. Total Float
   Float (or Slack) is the amount of time that an activity can be delayed without affecting the project end date.

The formula is shown below:

Total float = LS - ES

Total float = LF - EF

4. Free Float
   The amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the earliest start of any subsequent activity.

The formula is shown below:

Free Float = ESs - EF

Where ESs is the successor activity.

5. Three point estimation (Beta distribution)
   Three point estimating is a project management technique used to estimate the duration or cost of an activity by considering three scenarios: optimistic (O), pessimistic (P), and the most likely (M). Three point estimating uses two forms of distribution; Beta (PERT) distribution and triangular distribution.

PERT (Beta) distribution

This formula provides a weighted average, giving more importance to the most likely estimate. This is preferred for more accurate and smooth estimates, reflecting the central tendency of the data.

Activity duration = (P + 4M + O)/6

P = pessimistic value (worst-case scenario)

M = most likely value (expected scenario)

O = optimistic value (best-case scenario)

Project estimation is a vital part of project management, where you predict the time, cost, and resources needed to finish a project. Getting these estimates right is key to the project's success, keeping stakeholders happy, and managing resources effectively. This reading discusses the important estimation techniques and provides their formulas with examples.

Important estimation techniques
Analogous estimation
Analogous estimation is a technique where a project manager looks at a previous project to estimate the time and cost of a current project by comparing key factors. This method becomes more accurate with more data and is straightforward to use, especially when project details are scarce. It provides quick, general estimates based on historical data, making it useful in the early stages of a project or when only a rough estimate is needed. Analogous estimation has four variations:

Ratio estimates: This method uses historical data and adjusts for factors like inflation to predict project costs or time.

Estimate ranges: Provides a range of possible costs or timelines to help manage client expectations.

Three-point estimate: Offers three optimistic, most likely, and pessimistic scenarios to provide a more balanced estimate.

Single point estimate: Uses exact values from past projects for quick, rough estimates.

Example
A film production company is bidding on two projects: a documentary about a local wildlife sanctuary and a short film about urban life. For the wildlife documentary, they use data from a previous project—a documentary about a national park—to make an initial estimate using analogous estimation. This method works well because they can compare similar factors. However, for the urban life short film, they don’t have relevant past data, making it more challenging to provide an accurate estimate.

Parametric estimation
Parametric estimation involves evaluating key aspects of a project, like time, cost, or resources, using historical data, market information, and expert judgment. This method can be used alone or alongside other estimation techniques and can be applied to the whole project or just parts of it.

Key features of parametric estimation include:

It relies on historical data and statistical analysis.

It uses mathematical formulas to generate estimates.

It often provides more accurate estimates than analogous methods.

Formula
E_Parametric = a_old / p_old \* p_curr

Where

E_parametric = parametric estimate

A_old = historic amount of cost or time

P_old = Historic value of parameter

P_curr = value of that parameter in the current project

Example
Let's consider the same film production example. The company needs to estimate the time required to produce a new documentary. Having completed several similar projects in the past, the project team will refer to their in-house database to review the durations of those projects. They will use a parametric estimate, with the production time per minute of the finished film serving as the input parameter. In previous projects, the production time of the finished film was 4 hours per minute. The new documentary is expected to be 40 minutes long. The calculation of the product time using parametric estimating will be as follows:

Estimated production time = 4 hours / 1 minute \* 40 minutes = 160 hours.

Therefore, the initial parametric estimate for the time required to produce a 40 min documentary movie is 160 hours.

Top-down estimation
Top-down estimation involves setting a project’s budget or timeline as a whole before breaking it down into smaller parts. In this approach, project managers create a broad timeline or budget without focusing on detailed specifics. This method is often combined with a Work Breakdown Structure (WBS), which helps break down more extensive deliverables into smaller tasks. The overall estimate is then passed to others who can assess the costs or timeframes for each individual component more precisely.

Example
A company executive might decide to launch a new software application to improve their service offerings. However, the executive might not be familiar with the detailed costs, processes, or time required for software development, such as coding, user interface design, or beta testing. Using a top-down approach, the executive can create an initial plan and estimate for the project. As the development team starts working, they can refine and adjust these top-down estimates based on actual costs and timelines as more specific requirements emerge during the project.

Bottom-up estimation
Bottom-up estimating determines a project's total cost, resource needs, or time requirements by adding up the individual costs, task durations, and resource requirements. Initially, this method might involve some educated guesses, but estimates become more accurate as the project progresses. This technique gives teams and project managers a detailed understanding of the project's costs. Project managers can create precise and thorough estimates by considering all relevant components.

Example
Let's apply the bottom-up approach to the same example. The company’s executive aims to launch a new software application within one year to enhance their service offerings. The project manager starts by breaking the project into detailed tasks, including market research, planning, design, coding, testing, and deployment. The manager then consults with the team and experts to estimate the time and cost needed for each task. These individual estimates are combined to create a comprehensive project timeline and budget. As the project moves forward, these estimates are refined based on actual performance and any new requirements that emerge.

Expert judgment
Expert judgment involves using the experience and expertise of the project manager and team to estimate the resources and time needed to complete a project. This technique is popular among seasoned project managers for its simplicity and speed, especially for projects similar to those they’ve done before.

In this approach, the team leverages its collective knowledge to set realistic deadlines and resource requirements. Consulting with experts or individuals with relevant project experience is a common practice. This method is often faster and seen as more reliable than complex calculations because people tend to trust those who have successfully handled similar projects.

A key advantage of expert judgment is its flexibility; it can be used in both top-down and bottom-up estimating methods. However, it's important to note that expert insights may not always perfectly match the specifics of the current project. In such cases, while expert opinions are valuable, they should be complemented with other estimation techniques to ensure accuracy.

What-if analysis
What-if analysis is a valuable tool in project management that helps project managers create realistic and accurate timelines by identifying potential risks and uncertainties that could affect the project's progress and outcomes.

This approach simulates different scenarios and explores the potential results of various decisions and events. For example, if a project manager is organizing a large-scale conference, they might use what-if analysis to anticipate and prepare for possible disruptions, like a venue cancellation, speaker unavailability, or sudden changes in attendee numbers. Considering these scenarios, the manager can develop strategies to mitigate these risks and ensure the event runs smoothly. What-if analysis also enhances decision-making by providing insight into the possible impacts of different choices. It allows project managers to assess how changes in project scope, timelines, or resources could affect the overall project, enabling more informed and strategic planning.

Three point estimation
Three-point estimation improves the accuracy of bottom-up estimates by calculating an average based on three different scenarios: optimistic, pessimistic, and most likely. Project managers can create more balanced and realistic project estimates by considering these variations. This method helps account for uncertainty and provides a more nuanced view of potential outcomes.

Conclusion
In project management, accurate estimation is vital to completing projects successfully, satisfying stakeholders, and allocating resources effectively. Different estimation techniques provide various ways to predict project timelines, costs, and resources. Each method has its own strengths and is suited to specific scenarios, allowing project managers to choose the best approach based on the project's complexity and unique requirements.
