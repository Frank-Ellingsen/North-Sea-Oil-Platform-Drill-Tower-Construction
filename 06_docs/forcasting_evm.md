Earned Value Management (EVM) and forecasting are performance measurement techniques used in the Monitoring and Controlling (M&C) phase of the project lifecycle. In this phase, completed project work from the Execution stage (actuals) is compared with the original project plan (baselines). These actions are performed as part of the Control Costs process within the M&C process group.

By comparing the planned baselines to the actual performance, EVM produces performance metrics, such as Cost Variance (CV), Schedule Variance (SV), Cost Performance Index (CPI), and Schedule Performance Index (SPI). Performance measurement gives insight into current project performance (how the project has performed up to the point of measurement).

The project can then assess these metrics using forecasting. For example, in the event of negative variances in CV and SV, a project manager can forecast (estimate) the additional cost and time needed to complete the remaining work. These forecasts can help predict future performance from the current performance and trends, and inform the implementation of corrective measures required to ensure the project meets the forecasted metrics.

Key forecasting components
Forecasting involves estimating any additional cost and schedule needed for project completion at the time of performance measurement. The key components of forecasting are Estimate at Completion (EAC), Estimate to Completion (ETC), Variance at Complete (VAC), and To-Complete Performance Index (TCPI).

Estimate at Completion (EAC)
Definition: EAC is a forecast or revised estimation of the total project cost until completion, based on current performance trends (CPI and SPI) calculated in EVM.

Understanding: EAC It the new or revised Budget at Completion (BAC) following variance analysis, where BAC is the sum of all Planned Values. In other words, it is the revised overall budget allocation, updating the baseline for project performance in the initial project plan.

Importance: EAC helps project managers to predict future financial needs and to make necessary adjustments to stay within budget.

Estimate to Completion (ETC)
Definition: ETC is a forecast of the remaining cost required to complete the remaining project work from the variance analysis stop point (which is sometimes referred to as the intermediate stage).

Understanding: It represents the new or revised cost estimation of the remaining project work following the variance analysis.

Importance: ETC provides insights into future resource requirements and aids in planning for the remaining project activities.

Variance at Completion (VAC)
Definition: VAC is the difference between the original cost estimate, BAC, and the newly forecasted EAC.

Understanding: It measures variance between budget estimations at the start of the project and after the variance analysis.

Importance: VAC highlights potential budget overruns or savings, allowing project managers to take proactive measures. It also provides insights into the level of accuracy of the initial estimates, which can be included as lessons learned for future projects.

To-Complete Performance Index (TCPI)
Definition: TCPI measures the performance level that must be achieved on the remaining work to meet a specified goal (BAC or EAC).

Understanding: It is the forecast representing the target performance rate needed for the rest of the project to be completed within the budget estimates made.

Importance: TCPI helps project managers understand the level of efficiency required to meet project goals and adjust strategies accordingly to ensure this.

Forecasting formulas
Whenever there is a variance between a project’s performance in terms of cost and schedule from the original project plan, like the situation in the above graph, the project manager should forecast the required cost and schedule to complete the project. Forecasting means arriving at an estimation of cost and schedule for the future (pending or remaining) work in the project.

EAC, ETC, VAC, and TCPI are estimates used for forecasting future financial performance. An outline of each of these and the formulas for their calculations are provided below.

Estimate at Completion (EAC)
EAC is a revised estimation of the overall project cost including the future work. It can be calculated in three different ways or methods using the formulas below. The method and formula you use to calculate EAC will be project-specific, depending on the project context and other project factors.

Method 1a
Calculates EAC based on a totally fresh estimate of the remaining work using a bottom-up estimation method.

Formula 1a: EAC = AC + Bottom-up ETC

EAC is the sum of the cost of work completed (AC) and the cost estimation of the remaining work (ETC), calculated using bottom-up estimation.

Method 1b
Calculates EAC based on the original estimate (BAC) and the work completed (EV)

Formula 1b: EAC = AC + (BAC – EV)

EAC is the sum of the cost of work completed (AC) and the cost estimation of the remaining work (ETC), calculated by removing the past work value (EV) from the original estimate (BAC).

Method 2
Calculates EAC if the current cost performance (CPI) continues for the future work

Formula 2: EAC = BAC / CPI

EAC is the original overall project cost (BAC) divided by the current cost performance rate (CPI).

Method 3
Calculates EAC if the current cost and schedule performance continue for the future work

Formula 3: EAC = AC + [(BAC – EV) / (CPI * SPI)]

EAC is the sum of the cost of work completed (AC) and the cost of the remaining work (original total budget (BAC) minus the work value (EV)) divided by the product of the current cost and schedule performance rates (CPI and SPI).

Estimate to Complete (ETC)
ETC is a revised estimation of the costs of the remaining or pending project work.

Formula: ETC = EAC – AC

ETC is the difference between the cost of the past work (AC) and the overall revised estimate (EAC)

Variance at Completion (VAC)
VAC is the difference between the originally planned budget (BAC) and the estimated total cost at project completion based on current performance (EAC).

Formula: VAC = BAC – EAC

VAC > 0, indicates the project is expected to finish under budget

VAC < 0, indicates that the project is expected to finish over budget

To-Complete Performance Index (TCPI)
The TCPI indicates the cost efficiency or rate at which to complete the remaining project work within the specified budget, whether based on the BAC or EAC. While the two TCPI formulas are listed below, they will be discussed in-depth in the next lesson. The formula used depends on the project circumstances. It is specifically used to determine the cost rate at which the remaining project work should be performed to meet the original BAC or the forecasted EAC.

Formula 1: TCPI = (BAC – EV) / (BAC – AC)

Formula 2: TCPI = (BAC – EV) / (EAC – AC)

They can use these forecasts to manage these deviations, in other words, for effective cost management. Forecasting metrics
EAC
The Estimate at Completion (EAC) is the projected total cost to complete the project. It is estimated based on current performance measured at the status date. The endpoint of the Actual Costs (AC) curve represents this.

EAC is the sum of the actual costs (AC) incurred for the completed work up to the status date) and the estimated cost of the remaining work, also known as the Estimate to Complete (ETC), this is why EAC is expressed as: EAC = AC + ETC.

ETC
The Estimate to Complete (ETC) is the total estimated cost required to complete the remaining project work from the status date. The Estimate at Completion (EAC) represents the total cost expected for the project at its completion. You learned that EAC = AC + ETC. Therefore, to calculate the ETC, you need to subtract the actual costs (AC) from the new estimate of total project cost (EAC). In other words, the ETC is the difference between the EAC and AC, expressed as: ETC = EAC – AC.

VAC
The Variance at Completion (VAC) is another forecasting metric in EVM. It is the projected cost value by which the project will be over or under the planned budget, indicated by the distance between the EAC and BAC points on the graph. It is the difference between the original (BAC) and new (EAC) estimates of overall project costs, expressed as: VAC = BAC – EAC.

EAC and ETC forecasting with an example project
Let’s work through a real-world example to consolidate your understanding of the forecasting metrics EAC and ETC, and how to apply them.

Project scenario
You are given the following mid-project EVM report summary documenting the findings of a Variance Analysis of a company's year-end fundraiser, the Annual Charity Gala.

Project Data

$100,000

40 Weeks

50%

40%

Performance Indicators

Cost Variance (CV)

-$20,000

Schedule Variance (SV)

-$10,000

Cost Performance Index (CPI)

0.66

Schedule Performance Index (SPI)

0.8

Forecasting formulas
There are various methods to calculate EAC and ETC:

EAC
Method 1: EAC = AC + Bottom-up ETC or EAC = AC + (BAC – EV)

Method 2: EAC = BAC / CPI

Method 3: EAC = AC + (BAC – EV) / (CPI \* SPI)

ETC
Method 1: ETC = EAC – AC

Method 2: ETC = Bottom-up ETC

To determine which methods and formulas to use, you will need to consider the project context.

Based on the recommendations, you need to forecast future cost performance using the current performance rates. In other words, the remaining project work is expected to perform at the same efficiency rates (CPI and SPI are both < 1). This indicates that the project’s future work will also show variances from the original estimates (BAC) and will require new estimates to reach completion. Therefore, it is best to apply Method 3 to calculate EAC based on the current performance rates of CPI and SPI.

The same applies to ETC calculations. While EAC is forecasted for the entire project, ETC is forecasted for the remaining project work that needs to be completed. Both EAC and ETC are forecasted based on the current performance rates of CPI and SPI. Hence, ETC, the cost of remaining work, is calculated from EAC by removing the cost of completed work (AC), which aligns with Method 1.

Calculations
EAC = AC + (BAC – EV) / (CPI _ SPI) = $60,000 + [($100,000 – $40,000) / (0.6 _ 0.8)] = $185,000

ETC = EAC – AC = $185,000 – $60,000 = $125,000

As a Project Manager, you are curious to know the Variance at Completion (VAC). This will help give you insight into the variance or gap between the original (BAC) and the revised (EAC) estimations made.

To calculate VAC, you apply the formula,

VAC = BAC – EAC = $100,000 – $185,000 = -$85,000

Inferences
The new overall cost estimation for the entire project (EAC) is $185,000, and the cost needed to complete the remaining project work (ETC) is $125,000. The estimated difference in cost at the completion of the project, the Variance at Complete (VAC), is -$85,000.

From this, you now know that the project can be completed at a new budget of $185,000 (EAC) if it is completed at the current cost and schedule performance rates. In addition, at the time of completion, the estimated variance (VAC) is -$85,000, which reflects a huge gap in the BAC and EAC estimates and indicates a significant increase in cost compared to the original estimate.

The gap could be caused by either a faulty original estimate (BAC) or inefficiency in the project team's execution, which resulted in a large variance. It is the project manager's responsibility to investigate the real cause and document the lessons learned to avoid such impacts in future projects.

Conclusion
EVM forecasting is the process of reestimating the project cost after determining the project’s performance rates and variances through performance measurement. Forecasting involves different methods of calculating budget estimations for the entire project (EAC) and for the remaining work to be completed (ETC). EVM and forecasting help the project managers and the project management team make informed decisions regarding budget investments in the project, manage costs more effectively, and promote better project governance.
Introduction
The To-Complete Performance Index (TCPI) is a predictive metric within Earned Value Management (EVM) that informs project managers about the efficiency required to complete a project within a given financial constraint. TCPI is essential for understanding whether current performance levels can be maintained or need improvement to meet the budgetary goals. Serving as an early warning system, it helps project managers take proactive corrective measures.

Calculating TCPI
Let’s explore TCPI using a graph to illustrate the core concepts of Earned Value Measurement.

S-curve graph of project cost over time; visualizing key EVM forecasting components and metrics
In the graph shown, TCPI is calculated for the project activities on the right side of the status date line, which denotes the remaining activities in the project.

TCPI can be calculated using two different methods, depending on whether the goal is to

meet the original planned project budget (Budget at Completion or BAC) or

revised an estimate (Estimate at Completion or EAC).

In this graph, the BAC is indicated by the endpoint of the Planned Value (PV) curve, which represents the work planned to be done by the status date. EAC, which is a prediction of the total cost of the project based on the current performance, is represented by the endpoint of the Actual Cost (AC) curve.

Let’s explore two methods to calculate TCPI in EVM.

Method 1: TCPI using BAC
When EVM reveals that a project’s BAC is still valid until project completion, then TCPI can be calculated for the remaining project work based on the project’s BAC.

The formula to calculate TCPI based on BAC is TCPI = (BAC – EV) / (BAC – AC).

The Cost Performance Index (CPI), a metric from EVM variance analysis, is calculated by dividing EV and AC (CPI = EV / AC), offering insight into the cost efficiency of the completed work. TCPI is also calculated by dividing EV and AC, but only after removing the work performed so far because TCPI is meant for future activities in the project, forecasting the required cost efficiency of the remaining work to meet budget outcomes.

Let’s unpack the two components of the formula.

BAC – EV
On the graph, this is the vertical distance between the endpoint of the EV curve on the status date line and the BAC. EV denotes the value created by the work completed (past work), which, when removed from the original budget (BAC), gives the value to be generated by the remaining work (future work).

BAC – AC
On the graph, this is the vertical distance between the AC curve and the Budget at Completion (BAC). AC denotes the money spent on the work completed (past work), which when removed from the original budget (BAC) is the remaining cost to be spent on the remaining work (future work).

Thus, future EV divided by future AC based on the BAC is the To-Complete Performance Index (TCPI).

TCPI = Future EV / Future AC
Method 2: TCPI using BAC
When EVM reveals that the project will have a new EAC for completion, TCPI can be calculated for the remaining project work based on that EAC.

The formula to calculate TCPI based on EAC is TCPI = (BAC – EV) / (EAC – AC). As with Method 1, TCPI is calculated by taking the ratio of EV / AC after removing the past work from the new estimate (EAC), because TCPI is meant for the future project activities.

Let’s unpack the two components of the formula.

BAC – EV
This remains the same as in Method 1. It is the value of the remaining work based on the original budget, represented by the vertical distance between the endpoint of the EV curve and the BAC. The formula still considers removing past work (EV) from BAC because the work completed had BAC as its base estimate.

EAC – AC
On the graph, this value is the vertical distance between the endpoint of the AC curve on the status date line and the EAC. AC denotes the money spent for the work completed (past work), which, when removed from the newly estimated budget (EAC), is the remaining cost to be spent for the remaining work (future work). This is equivalent to the Estimate to complete (ETC) formula, which you learned about previously (ETC = EAC – AC). Here, you will subtract AC from EAC because the future work is going to be dependent on the new estimate (EAC).

Thus, the ratio of the future EV and future AC based on the EAC is the To-Complete Performance Index (TCPI).

TCPI calculation using an example
Consider a construction project to build a commercial complex with the following parameters:

Budget at Completion (BAC): $5,000,000

Duration: 24 months

The project is stopped at the end of the 12th month to measure the parameters, which are:

Earned Value (EV): $2,200,000

Actual Cost (AC): $2,500,000

Method 1
Despite the differences between EV and AC, assuming the construction company wants to complete the project within the original estimate (BAC), the TCPI for the rest of the project will be:

TCPI = (BAC – EV) / (BAC – AC)

           = ($5,000,000 – $2,200,000) / ($5,000,000 – $2,500,000)

           = $2,800,000 / $2,500,000 = 1.12

As TCPI is measured to be greater than 1, the project is in a challenging position. The project team must work 12% more efficiently to complete the remaining work so that the original budget (BAC) can be achieved at the end of the project.

Method 2
At 50% project completion, the PV is $2,500,000 (50% of BAC) but the EV generated was only $2,200,000. This indicates a Schedule Variance (SV) of -$300,000 (SV = EV – PV) and a Cost Variance (CV) of -$300,000 (CV = EV – AC).

As the project has deviated both in terms of cost and schedule, the construction company has decided to proceed with a revised estimate, an EAC of $5,500,000.

The TCPI for the rest of the project under the new estimate (EAC) will be:

TCPI = (BAC – EV) / (EAC – AC)

           = ($5,000,000 –$2,200,000) / ($5,500,000 –$2,500,000)

           = $2,800,000 / $3,000,000 = 0.93

As the TCPI is measured to be less than 1, the project is in a favorable position. The project team can work 7% less efficiently than planned so that the revised budget (EAC) can be achieved at the end of the project.

Insights and actions from TCPI
To respond to the insights gained from TCPI, project managers can take informed, strategic actions to ensure the project is aligned with its objectives and stays on track for successful completion.

Resource reallocation
To achieve the required efficiency indicated by TCPI based on BAC, the project manager might reallocate resources, such as assigning additional skilled labor to critical tasks or investing in more efficient machinery.

Process improvements
The project manager could identify and implement process improvements, such as optimizing supply chain logistics to reduce delays or enhancing coordination among subcontractors to minimize rework.

Cost management
Given the EAC-based TCPI, the project manager can focus on effective cost management strategies, ensuring that any additional expenditures are justified and provide value to the project.

Stakeholder communication
The project manager regularly communicates TCPI insights to stakeholders, explaining the required performance levels and actions taken to achieve them. This transparency helps maintain stakeholder confidence and support.

Guiding corrective actions
TCPI provides a clear indication of the required performance levels, guiding project managers in taking timely corrective actions. For instance, if the TCPI is above 1, it signals the need for performance improvements. Conversely, a TCPI below 1 indicates that the project is on track or performing better than expected, allowing the manager to allocate resources more flexibly.

Facilitating informed decision-making
By integrating TCPI with other EVM metrics, project managers gain a comprehensive understanding of project health. This holistic view enables informed decision-making, ensuring that actions are based on accurate and timely data. For example, if both TCPI and Cost Performance Index (CPI) indicate poor performance, a detailed review and significant corrective measures may be necessary.

Enhancing stakeholder confidence
Transparent communication of TCPI and its implications fosters stakeholder confidence. By providing stakeholders with clear, quantifiable insights into project performance and required future efficiency, project managers can build trust and ensure alignment on project goals and expectations. Regular updates on TCPI and other EVM metrics keep stakeholders informed and engaged, reducing uncertainties and fostering a collaborative project environment.

The role of TCPI in the project lifecycle
TCPI is a crucial forecasting metric in EVM that project managers can use to guide decisions and measure performance evaluation throughout each project phase, from planning to closure.

Initial planning phase
During the project planning phase, the project manager establishes the baseline budget (BAC) and schedules, setting the stage for future performance measurement. TCPI is introduced as a key performance indicator that will be monitored throughout the project lifecycle. This early introduction ensures all team members and stakeholders understand its importance and implications.

Regular reporting and monitoring
TCPI is reported during regular project performance reviews, typically on a monthly basis or at key project milestones. These reviews involve comparing actual performance against planned performance, calculating EVM metrics, and assessing TCPI. Regular reporting includes:

Monthly status reports: Including TCPI calculations based on both BAC and EAC, along with narratives explaining the implications and any necessary actions.

Quarterly reviews: Conducting more in-depth analyses of performance trends and forecasting future performance based on TCPI and other EVM metrics.

Mid-project evaluations
At the project’s midpoint, a comprehensive evaluation is performed to assess overall progress and adjust forecasts as needed. TCPI plays a crucial role in these evaluations, providing insights into whether the project can still meet its original budget or if a revised estimate (EAC) is more realistic. Based on TCPI, the project manager may recommend adjustments to project plans, resource allocations, or scope to ensure financial objectives are met.

Final stages and closure
As the project approaches completion, TCPI continues to guide performance assessments. During the final stages, the focus shifts from achieving financial targets to ensuring deliverables meet quality standards and stakeholder expectations. TCPI is included in the final project performance reports, providing a comprehensive view of how efficiently the project was managed financially.

Conclusion
The TCPI is a vital component of EVM, providing project managers with predictive insights into the required performance efficiency to achieve budgetary goals. By understanding and applying TCPI, project managers can take proactive measures to improve performance, manage risks, make informed decisions, and maintain stakeholder confidence. Regular monitoring and reporting of TCPI ensure that projects stay on track financially and that any deviations are addressed promptly. In summary, TCPI is an indispensable tool for achieving successful project outcomes and ensuring efficient use of resources throughout the project lifecycle.
